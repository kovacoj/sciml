The one-dimensional quantum harmonic oscillator is a compact test of how neural and finite-element methods recover an ordered spectrum:

$$
\left(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\d^2}{\d{x}^2}+\frac{1}{2}x^2\right)\psi_n=E_n\psi_n,
\qquad E_n=n+\frac12.
$$

<Callout title="Precomputed experiment">
  Firedrake and PyTorch do not run in this page. The browser loads a versioned JSON dataset produced by the experiment script. Solver values, errors, timings, and sampled eigenfunctions are therefore reproducible static results rather than a client-side reimplementation. Direct FE is used as the numerical reference for the browser comparison. The known analytic harmonic-oscillator eigenpairs remain an independent validation target.
</Callout>

## Compare eigenstates

Select a neural formulation. All eigenstates available for both the selected
method and the Direct FE reference are displayed simultaneously. Each pair is
placed on the corresponding Direct FE energy level, so the solid and dashed
curves can be compared directly. Neutral marks show the Direct FE energy
ladder, while colored dashed marks show the selected method's computed
eigenvalues.

def prepare_eigenstate_pairs_ho(
    reference_states,
    selected_states,
    coordinates,
):
    def normalize(values):
        array = np.asarray(values, dtype=float)

        norm_squared = float(
            np.trapezoid(
                array**2,
                coordinates,
            )
        )

        norm = np.sqrt(
            max(norm_squared, 1.0e-15)
        )

        return array / norm

    reference_rows = []
    selected_rows = []
    reference_energies = []
    selected_energies = []

    for reference_state, selected_state in zip(
        reference_states,
        selected_states,
        strict=True,
    ):
        reference_psi = normalize(
            reference_state["values"]
        )

        selected_psi = normalize(
            selected_state["values"]
        )

        overlap = float(
            np.trapezoid(
                reference_psi * selected_psi,
                coordinates,
            )
        )

        if overlap < 0.0:
            selected_psi = -selected_psi

        reference_rows.append(reference_psi)
        selected_rows.append(selected_psi)

        reference_energies.append(
            float(reference_state["eigenvalue"])
        )

        selected_energies.append(
            float(selected_state["eigenvalue"])
        )

    return (
        np.asarray(reference_rows),
        np.asarray(selected_rows),
        np.asarray(reference_energies),
        np.asarray(selected_energies),
    )

## Method descriptions

### Direct FE reference

Direct FE assembles the CG2 Hamiltonian and mass matrices with Firedrake, then solves the generalized eigenproblem with SciPy. It provides a high-fidelity numerical reference against which the neural methods are compared; its accuracy is ultimately limited by the finite-element discretization and solver tolerance.

### Weak neural formulation

The weak neural method begins from the stationary Schrödinger eigenproblem

$$
\left(
-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm d^2}{\mathrm d x^2}
+\frac{1}{2}x^2
\right)\psi
=E\psi,
$$

but it does not enforce this equation pointwise. Instead, the infinite domain is first truncated to

$$
\Omega=(-L,L),
$$

and homogeneous Dirichlet conditions are imposed,

$$
\psi(-L)=\psi(L)=0.
$$

We multiply the differential equation by a test function $v\in H_0^1(\Omega)$ and integrate over the domain:

$$
-\frac12\int_\Omega \psi''v\,\mathrm dx
+
\frac12\int_\Omega x^2\psi v\,\mathrm dx
=E\int_\Omega \psi v\,\mathrm dx.
$$

Integrating the second-order term by parts gives

$$
\frac12\int_\Omega \psi'v'\,\mathrm dx
+
\frac12\int_\Omega x^2\psi v\,\mathrm dx
=E\int_\Omega \psi v\,\mathrm dx
\qquad
\forall v\in H_0^1(\Omega).
$$

The boundary contribution vanishes because the test functions are zero at $x=\pm L$. Defining

$$
a(u,v)
:=
\frac12\int_\Omega u'v'\,\mathrm dx
+
\frac12\int_\Omega x^2uv\,\mathrm dx
$$

and

$$
m(u,v)
:=
\int_\Omega uv\,\mathrm dx,
$$

the eigenproblem becomes

$$
a(\psi,v)=E\,m(\psi,v)
\qquad
\forall v\in H_0^1(\Omega).
$$

This is the weak formulation. It requires only first derivatives of the trial and test functions, whereas the strong PINN formulation requires the second derivative of the neural approximation.

#### Finite-element trial and test spaces

The interval is partitioned into a finite-element mesh,

$$
-L=x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_N=L.
$$

Two finite-dimensional spaces are then selected:

$$
U_h\subset H_0^1(\Omega)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
V_h\subset H_0^1(\Omega).
$$

In the reported experiment, the neural trial function is represented in a quadratic continuous finite-element space,

$$
U_h=\mathrm{CG}_2,
$$

while the equation is tested against the enriched cubic space

$$
V_h=\mathrm{CG}_3.
$$

Because the trial and test spaces differ, this is an enriched Petrov–Galerkin formulation. Let

$$
\{\phi_j\}_{j=1}^{N_U}
$$

be a basis of $U_h$, and let

$$
\{v_i\}_{i=1}^{N_V}
$$

be a basis of $V_h$. The discrete trial function has the form

$$
u_{\theta,h}(x)
=
\sum_{j=1}^{N_U}c_j(\theta)\phi_j(x).
$$

A coordinate multilayer perceptron is evaluated at the trial-space degrees of freedom to produce the coefficient vector

$$
c_\theta=
\begin{bmatrix}
c_1(\theta)&\cdots&c_{N_U}(\theta)
\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T}.
$$

The network therefore does not directly produce a continuous function at arbitrary collocation points. It produces the coefficients of a finite-element function. Firedrake supplies the mesh, basis functions, quadrature, boundary treatment, and discrete differential operators. This is the optimization scaffold referred to in the page's current method description.

#### Discrete weak residual

Testing the equation against every basis function $v_i$ produces one residual component per test-space direction:

$$
r_i(\theta,E)
=
a(u_{\theta,h},v_i)
-
E\,m(u_{\theta,h},v_i).
$$

Firedrake assembles the corresponding trial-to-test matrices

$$
H_{VU,ij}
=
\frac12\int_\Omega
\phi_j'v_i'\,\mathrm dx
+
\frac12\int_\Omega
x^2\phi_jv_i\,\mathrm dx
$$

and

$$
M_{VU,ij}
=
\int_\Omega \phi_jv_i\,\mathrm dx.
$$

The complete residual vector is therefore

$$
r_\theta
=
H_{VU}c_\theta
-
E_\theta M_{VU}c_\theta.
$$

Each entry measures how strongly the approximate eigenpair violates the equation in one finite-element test direction. Driving the full vector toward zero approximates the requirement that the weak equation hold for every admissible test function.

The eigenvalue associated with the current trial function is obtained from its Rayleigh quotient,

$$
E_\theta
=

## H_{VU}c_\theta

E_\theta M_{VU}c_\theta.
$$

Each entry measures how strongly the approximate eigenpair violates the equation in one finite-element test direction. Driving the full vector toward zero approximates the requirement that the weak equation hold for every admissible test function.

The eigenvalue associated with the current trial function is obtained from its Rayleigh quotient,

$$
E_\theta
=
\frac{
c_\theta^{\mathsf T}H_Uc_\theta
}{
c_\theta^{\mathsf T}M_Uc_\theta
},
$$

where $H_U$ and $M_U$ are the Hamiltonian and mass matrices assembled on the trial space.

#### Measuring the residual in the test-space dual norm

The Euclidean norm $\lVert r_\theta\rVert_2$ depends on the scaling of the chosen test basis. Instead, the residual is measured in a norm induced by the test-function space.

For the scaled $H^1$ test norm, the Gram matrix has the form

$$
G_V=M_V+h^2K_V,
$$

where

$$
(M_V)_{ij}
=
\int_\Omega v_jv_i\,\mathrm dx
$$

and

$$
(K_V)_{ij}
=
\int_\Omega v_j'v_i'\,\mathrm dx.
$$

The corresponding discrete dual norm is

$$
\lVert r_\theta\rVert_{V_h^\ast}^2
=
r_\theta^{\mathsf T}G_V^{-1}r_\theta.
$$

Computationally, this is evaluated by solving

$$
G_Vz_\theta=r_\theta
$$

and forming

$$
\lVert r_\theta\rVert_{V_h^\ast}^2
=
r_\theta^{\mathsf T}z_\theta.
$$

The experiment supplements this weak residual with an $L^2$-type residual contribution,

$$
r_\theta^{\mathsf T}M_V^{-1}r_\theta,
$$

so that residual components suppressed by the weaker dual norm are still penalized. Schematically, the training objective is

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{weak}}
=
E_\theta
+
\beta
\left(
r_\theta^{\mathsf T}G_V^{-1}r_\theta
+
\gamma\,
r_\theta^{\mathsf T}M_V^{-1}r_\theta
\right).
$$

The residual terms encourage satisfaction of the weak eigenvalue equation, while the energy term selects the lowest admissible eigenstate.

#### Higher eigenstates

To recover the spectrum sequentially, the candidate for state $n$ is projected onto the mass-orthogonal complement of the previously accepted states. If

$$
\Psi=
\begin{bmatrix}
c_0&c_1&\cdots&c_{n-1}
\end{bmatrix},
$$

then a raw neural coefficient vector $z_\theta$ is projected as

$$
\widetilde c_\theta
=
z_\theta
-
\Psi
\left(
\Psi^{\mathsf T}M_U\Psi
\right)^{-1}
\Psi^{\mathsf T}M_Uz_\theta.
$$

It is subsequently normalized in the finite-element $L^2$ inner product:

$$
c_\theta
=
\frac{\widetilde c_\theta}
{
\sqrt{
\widetilde c_\theta^{\mathsf T}
M_U
\widetilde c_\theta
}
}.
$$

Consequently,

$$
c_i^{\mathsf T}M_Uc_j=\delta_{ij},
$$

up to numerical precision. The energy selector then seeks the lowest-energy state remaining in this orthogonal complement.

#### Differentiability

Although Firedrake assembles the operators, the optimization remains differentiable with respect to the neural-network parameters. The matrices

$$
H_{VU},\quad M_{VU},\quad G_V,\quad H_U,\quad M_U
$$

are fixed tensors during training. PyTorch differentiates through:

* the coordinate MLP;
* the finite-element coefficient vector;
* mass projection and normalization;
* matrix-vector products;
* the Rayleigh quotient;
* and the linear solves defining the residual norm.

No second coordinate derivatives of the neural network are required. The differential operator is represented by preassembled finite-element matrices, while the network supplies a differentiable map from coordinates to trial-space coefficients.

### Strong PINN

Strong PINN minimizes the pointwise differential-equation residual using second coordinate derivatives. No finite-element assembly is required, but the optimization landscape is less constrained.

### Variational Rayleigh-Ritz

Variational Rayleigh-Ritz minimizes the neural Rayleigh quotient subject to projection against previously learned states. Sequential deflation in this setting can propagate inaccurate earlier states into later eigenstates.

## Interpretation

### Eigenvalue accuracy

Each solid Direct FE eigenfunction and its dashed neural counterpart are placed
on the same Direct FE energy baseline. The colored dashed horizontal marks
independently show the eigenvalues computed by the selected neural method.

### Eigenfunction accuracy

When a dashed curve overlaps its solid Direct FE reference, the selected method
has recovered that eigenfunction's shape. Because each state is shown at its
own energy level, the entire spectrum can be inspected without different modes
being superimposed around zero.

### Timing

Computation times describe one workstation and reflect algorithmic overhead, architecture choices, and optimizer budgets. They should be compared relatively.

## Data and provenance

The page dataset lives at [`harmonic-oscillator-comparison.json`](../../../data/harmonic-oscillator-comparison.json). Keeping this compact artifact beside the site source has three advantages over a transient CI artifact: every deployment has a stable URL, changes are reviewable in Git, and the page remains functional after workflow artifacts expire.

The JSON records the generating command, source script, coordinate grid, eigenvalues, sampled normalized eigenfunctions, state-wise errors, and training times. Regenerate it when solver settings or implementations change; do not edit numerical values by hand.

## Limitations

- The curves are samples of precomputed finite-element or neural functions, not live solves.
- Timing values describe one machine and should be interpreted comparatively.
- Sequential neural deflation can propagate inaccurate earlier states into later states.
- A compact grid is used for browser delivery; full checkpoints and assembled matrices remain outside the website repository.
